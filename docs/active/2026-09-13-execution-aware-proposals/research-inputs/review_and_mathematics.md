## 4. Why “structured” does not imply a small expected count

A controller with a single retry operation and one goal state is highly structured. If it succeeds with probability p per attempt, it has expected count 1/p. At p = 1/1,400,000, that is 1.4 million actions. With per-attempt cost 0.153, its expected cost is 214,200. If no other action is available, it is the optimum of that toy problem.

This does not excuse the actual Bow result; it prevents an invalid inference. A large count may reflect a needless rare-event strategy, a hidden useful option, an unsuitable decomposition, or a genuinely rare required event. Inspect the real native opportunity rather than assume count is proof of a bug.

Nor does a mean specify finite-limit completion. Deterministic 1000 actions and a distribution that takes one action with probability 0.999 and 999001 actions with probability 0.001 both have mean 1000. Their chance of finishing within 100 actions is completely different. Keep actual finite-run qualification separate; do not infer quantiles from the mean or fit a geometric law without establishing its reset semantics.

## 5. Two rewards on the same controller

For a fixed finite controller, include every relevant physical state, program counter and observation/checkpoint state. Let P contain nonterminal transitions. Let c_i be expected native monetary cost incurred at decision i and n_i its expected number of primitive executions before the next represented decision. Assume all selected transitions are complete, the entry-reachable controller is proper, and local rewards are finite.

Then the transient fundamental matrix F = (I-P)^(-1) exists, and

    C = c + P C = F c
    N = n + P N = F n.

This is the existing fixed-policy reward argument applied to two right-hand sides. It does not require statistical independence between internal operation counts and the exit: linearity of expectation already gives the stopped-process equation. It does require the same committed controller and complete conditional outcome law.

For a primitive decision, the appropriate n follows its actual execution semantics. For an option, n is the native expected internal primitive action count, not 1. `OptionKernel` already stores `expected_primitive_actions`. Its first-exit/retry normalization must match the row: charging the option's full until-success duration and also normalizing another retry would double-count. Conversely, counting only one macro call could hide arbitrarily long work. [R6]

Root occupancy d satisfies d^T = e_root^T F. Immediate totals are C(root) = sum_i d_i c_i and N(root) = sum_i d_i n_i. A state's C_i or N_i includes its future; summing d_i C_i or d_i N_i counts overlapping future work repeatedly. A currency resource count can also differ from primitive executions: several currency units can be consumed by one operation, and a price key can be shared by distinct operations.

The transition structure is reusable under a changed proposal weighting when its native identity is unchanged. Fixed-policy count is price-independent, but a changed price or weight may change the selected policy. Old values or lower feasibility cannot then be reused as if the controller/objective were identical.

## 6. Count-biased proposals without replacing the cost objective

For a fixed policy and lambda >= 0,

    G_lambda(pi) = C(pi) + lambda N(pi).

Lambda has units of Chaos per primitive execution. In this programme it is an exploration parameter, not a claim about the user's value of time. A few weighted objectives can propose controllers that do not repeatedly revisit cheap but difficult acquisition attempts.

This is related to scalarisation in constrained SSPs, but implementing a handful of proposed policies is not solving a constrained SSP, enumerating the Pareto frontier, or importing CARL's optimality result. Our original public objective remains C. [E1]

A synthetic counterexample makes the boundary unavoidable. Policy A succeeds with probability 0.01 per attempt at cost 0.01: C_A=1, N_A=100. Policy B finishes immediately for 2: C_B=2, N_B=1. At lambda=0.02, their scores are 3 and 2.02, so the proposer favors B. But 2.02 is not a lower on the original optimal cost, which is 1. A more comfortable execution can be more expensive.

For exact minimizers over an unchanged complete feasible set, increasing lambda cannot increase the selected N (with ordinary consistent tie handling). Subtract the two optimality inequalities to obtain this. Bounded beam/local search does not inherit that monotonicity. Changing which actions become available also breaks that comparison premise.

A finite list of weights need not find every nondominated deterministic policy. The pairs (C,N)=(0,10),(6,6),(10,0) are all nondominated, but the middle pair would need both lambda >= 1.5 and lambda <= 2/3 to beat the extremes. Do not claim completeness of a tiny trade-off set. The selected task needs a few useful candidates, not that completeness guarantee.

The proposed implementation therefore retains true c and n separately, ranks or briefly improves the private view under c+lambda n, constructs all selected tails, and independently evaluates the actual graph at original c. Only the original-cost result can replace the current cost winner. A useful shorter/expensive alternative is a labelled trade-off. Let cost-only improvement subsequently exploit states/continuations discovered by the count-aware pass.

## 7. Count and the estimated-potential discussion

A count estimate can be as aggressive as a cost estimate when used only to rank proposals. A certified minimum-count lower instead requires inequalities

    h_N(s) <= n(s,a) + E[h_N(S')]

for every required original action/member, with the same goal, control and boundary conventions. Its units are actions. It does not imply a monetary lower unless an additional uniform reward relationship is proved.

For example, c(s,a) >= k n(s,a) for every required action, with k >= 0, would allow k h_N as a monetary lower. A fixed policy's average C/N is not such an all-action inequality. A cheap alternative can violate it. Cost partitioning can provide more expressive relationships, but this programme does not select another decomposition/proof project before the proposal experiment has a consumer.

A checked potential produced using c+lambda n certifies that modified model, not the original cost. Merely contracting or clipping it does not cure a missing action or native correspondence. The existing original-cost checker can test any proposed vector, but its unchanged cheap escape remains a ceiling. Keep that negative result durable.

Count also helps understand numerical sensitivity. For the same fixed proper controller and any vector v, define residual r=c+Pv-v. Then

    C-v = F r.

If a justified componentwise bound satisfies |r_i| <= epsilon n_i, nonnegativity of F gives

    |C-v| <= epsilon N.

A 1.4M-action controller can amplify per-action error substantially. This is a conditional error statement, not a reason to lower the evaluator's precision or evidence that its current answer is invalid. A uniform error bound on a learned value is not established here. The recent rollout literature makes a related conditional hitting-time observation. [E4]

## 8. Assessment of the supplied direction audit

**Agree:** aggressive proposal algorithms can be simpler and need not carry proof authority. Beam selection, incomplete heuristic models, sampled rankings or learned scores can decide what to try. The project already separates private values, native executable uppers and all-action lowers; use that rather than add a new planner layer.

**Necessary qualification:** a small controller is not necessarily a small verification problem. An option with a few explicit nodes can induce many physical item/control pairs. An upper checker must cover all selected outcomes. An optimality certificate additionally needs all originally allowed alternatives, explicit or soundly summarized, not merely the ones the proposal prefers.

The TACAS certificate work supports independent witness checking, but its model-construction boundary is explicit. It does not promise that a tiny proposal eliminates native model construction. [E2]

**Selected application:** cheaper, count-aware proposals at the missed Bow entry; complete native verification; continued cost-only improvement. Not selected: a replacement proof engine, broad lossy exact quotient, offline model-training pipeline, universal endgame library, or weakening acceptance to make a candidate pass.

The strongest objection is that the current long Bow route may be genuinely economically attractive, or that all shorter admitted routes are costlier. The three-arm experiment and original-price evaluation resolve this without changing the objective. A second objection is that completing candidate tails/checking remains expensive despite cheap ranking. If every attempt stops at the same semantic/verification barrier, fix that narrow owner or stop the proposal variant rather than make the guide more elaborate.
